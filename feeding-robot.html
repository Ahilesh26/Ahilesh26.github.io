Assistive Feeding Robot with Impedance Safety

Designing a robot that operates in direct physical contact with a human being is a fundamentally different engineering challenge from designing one that works in a factory cage. In a factory, a collision is a fault to be avoided. In assistive robotics, contact is the whole point — and the question is not whether the robot will touch the person, but how it will respond when it does.

The target population is individuals with upper-limb motor impairments — ALS, high cervical spinal cord injury, muscular dystrophy — where the person has full cognitive function but cannot bring food to their own mouth. Current fixed-trajectory feeding robots don't respond to the unexpected: a head movement, a spill, a user flinching. Impedance control changes that.

What Impedance Control Actually Means

A position-controlled robot follows a trajectory regardless of the forces it encounters — tell it to move to (x, y, z) and it will, even if there's a face in the way. An impedance-controlled robot instead regulates the relationship between force and displacement: it behaves like a virtual spring-damper system. If something pushes back, the robot yields in proportion to that force rather than fighting through it. You can make the impedance asymmetric — low stiffness on approach, higher stiffness at the delivery point.

System Architecture

The robot is a 6-DOF manipulator running a ROS 2 control stack. A wrist-mounted force/torque sensor provides contact feedback. A secondary safety layer monitors the sensor at 500 Hz and triggers a soft stop if any force component exceeds 5N. Food detection uses a top-down camera and colour-segmentation pipeline to locate scoopable regions on the plate.

The hardest design decision was choosing the impedance parameters. Too stiff and the robot doesn't yield meaningfully. Too compliant and the fork wobbles at every perturbation. I ended up with a gain schedule: lower stiffness during approach, higher stiffness at the final delivery position.
