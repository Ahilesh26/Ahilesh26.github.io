Articulated Underwater Buoyancy Robot

Underwater robotics is a domain where all the constraints hit at once — pressure, waterproofing, buoyancy, limited communications — and you have to solve them simultaneously. There's no staging. You can't debug half of it in air and half in water. This project started from a research question: how do you build a robot that can navigate and take measurements inside confined bodies of water — industrial tanks, reservoirs, flooded shafts — where conventional ROVs are too bulky to manoeuvre?

The answer was buoyancy-driven propulsion paired with an articulated body that can change direction without thrusters. Buoyancy-driven gliders use a bladder or piston system to change their volume — and therefore density — relative to the surrounding fluid. They're extraordinarily energy-efficient compared to propeller-driven ROVs, and they produce almost no turbulence — critical in environments where flow disturbance would corrupt measurements.

Conceptual Design

The chosen architecture is a three-segment body — a central buoyancy module flanked by two articulated tail sections. Each tail segment can rotate relative to the adjacent one, driven by a waterproofed servo. By coordinating these rotations the robot produces a gentle undulating motion that steers it left, right, or into a shallow banking turn — all without any thrusters creating disturbing flow.

The Articulated Joint

Each joint consists of a machined aluminium pivot pin running through two flanged segment caps, with a custom silicone gasket on the inner face. The servo sits inside the dry volume of one segment; its output shaft passes through the gasket-sealed bore to connect to the adjacent segment's cap. This keeps all electronics dry while allowing ±25° of rotation in the horizontal plane. After three prototype iterations, the final joint used a hollow pivot with the servo cable routed through the bore and potted at both exits with marine-grade epoxy.

Buoyancy Engine & Control

The buoyancy engine is a syringe-style piston driven by a small DC motor and leadscrew. When the piston retracts it draws water into a flexible external bladder, increasing the vehicle's volume without changing its mass — effective density drops below water and the robot rises. A depth sensor (pressure transducer) closes the loop: the controller maintains a target depth by modulating piston position while the tail servos steer.

Getting neutral buoyancy right took an embarrassingly long time. The robot is only neutrally buoyant when its total mass exactly equals the mass of the water it displaces — and that changes with water temperature, salinity, and any sensor payload you add. In the end I added a small trim weight tray that can be adjusted before each deployment.
